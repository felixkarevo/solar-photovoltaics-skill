# Electricity Basics for Solar Systems

For laypeople: this file explains the electrical fundamentals behind every solar design. Professionals: use it as the formula sheet. All formulas assume copper conductors unless stated.

## 1. The four quantities

| Symbol | Name | Unit | Meaning | Water analogy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| U | Voltage | V (volt) | Electrical pressure / potential difference | Pressure |
| I | Current | A (ampere) | Charge flow rate | Flow rate |
| R | Resistance | Ω (ohm) | Opposition to flow | Pipe narrowness |
| P | Power | W (watt) | Energy per second, P = U × I | Power of the jet |

- **Energy** = power × time. Unit: **Wh** (watt-hours), billed in **kWh** (1 kWh = 1000 Wh = 3.6 MJ). A 100 W lamp burning 3 h uses 300 Wh = 0.3 kWh.
- **Ohm's law**: U = R × I; P = U × I = I²R = U²/R.
- Conversion efficiency cannot exceed 100%. Real-world anchors (state of the art): resistance heating = 100%, electric motor 98%, generator 98%, combustion→electric in a steam plant ≤47%, combined cycle ≤60%; modern Li-ion battery round trip ≈ 95%, lead-acid ≈ 70–75%.

## 2. DC vs AC

- **DC (direct current)**: voltage polarity constant. Sources: PV panels, batteries, USB. Current equals load demand at the fixed voltage.
- **AC (alternating current)**: polarity reverses periodically. Sine wave, frequency f in Hz (cycles/second). Europe: 50 Hz; North/Central America, Japan (partly), Korea 60 Hz. Taiwan/Philippines 60 Hz. Japan has both 50 Hz (east) and 60 Hz (west) — why frequency converters exist.
- **Why AC dominates grids**: transformers change AC voltage cheaply and with almost no loss; DC cannot be transformed with simple equipment. High voltage → low current → low I²R transmission loss. Same logic as choosing 48 V over 12 V off-grid.
- **Full-DC systems were rejected historically** because all conventional rotating machines and transformers are induction devices that need AC.

## 3. AC values: peak, RMS, and waveform

- Instantaneous sine voltage: `u(t) = Û × sin(2πft)`, where Û is the **peak** value.
- **RMS (root-mean-square) = the effective value**: the DC voltage that delivers the same heating power. For a pure sine: `U_rms = Û / √2`, and `Û = U_rms × √2`.
- **Mains 230 V RMS (Europe, ±10% tolerance)**: peak ≈ 325 V. 60 Hz regions 120 V RMS (peak ≈ 170 V) or 230–240 V.
- Meters (multimeters) display RMS values; insulation and capacitor ratings must cover the peak.
- **Waveform quality**: pure sine = correct for everything. Modified square/sine wave = cheaper, causes extra heating in motors, buzz in audio, failures in some switch-mode supplies and microwaves. Off-grid recommendation: pure sine inverter.
- **Frequency matters to**: synchronous speeds of motors (a 50 Hz motor runs ~17% fast on 60 Hz), clock accuracy of cheap devices, and transformers (a transformer rated for 60 Hz overheats on 50 Hz; one rated for 50 Hz usually runs fine on 60 Hz), and inverter/grid matching. Grid-tie inverters sync to grid frequency exactly.

## 4. Three-phase AC (230/400 V)

- Three sinusoidal voltages, mutually offset by 120°, generated by three stator windings (phases R/S/T or U/V/W).
- **Single-phase households**: one phase + neutral, 230 V RMS (Europe).
- **Between any two phases**: 400 V RMS (230 × √3). Three-phase loads (motors, wallboxes ≥11 kW, heat pumps, workshop machines) use 400 V.
- Per-phase wire current is lower for the same power in three-phase: `I = P / (√3 × U × cos φ)` for balanced loads. This is why an 11 kW wallbox needs ~16 A per phase (400 V) but would need ~48 A single-phase.
- **Phase imbalance**: distribute single-phase loads across the three phases; a heavily loaded single phase causes neutral-conductor currents and voltage asymmetry — large PV systems are therefore connected in three-phase balance (e.g. 3-phase hybrid inverters feed equally into all phases).
- Voltage levels in the German/European grid (headroom rule: continuous voltage limit ≈ +20% above nominal): low voltage up to 1 kV; medium voltage 10–30 (70) kV; high voltage 110–170 kV; extra-high 220/380 (420) kV. HVDC (direct-current transmission) wins for very long distances/high powers because there is no skin effect and no reactive charging current.

## 5. Real, reactive, apparent power, power factor

- **Real/active power P (W)**: does the actual work (heat, light, torque).
- **Reactive power Q (var)**: sloshes back and forth between source and inductances/capacitances (motor windings, cable capacitance); does no work but loads the wires and the inverter.
- **Apparent power S (VA)**: what the source must actually deliver, `S = U × I` (single-phase), `S = P / cos φ`.
- **Power factor** `cos φ = P / S`. Pure resistive load (heater): cos φ = 1. Inductive (fridge compressor without electronics): 0.4–0.7. Modern electronics with PFC: >0.95.
- **Inverter sizing rule**: `Inverter VA rating ≥ Σ simultaneous loads (W) / cos φ`. For fridge + pump loads assume cos φ ≈ 0.65 unless datasheet says better.
- **Motors**: starting (locked-rotor) current 2–6× nominal for 1–3 seconds; the inverter's **surge rating** must cover this. Compressors (fridges, air-con) are the usual trip cause.
- Grid context: conventional synchronous generators historically supplied the grid's reactive power; inverter-based PV adds little unless designed to (modern grid codes now demand Q capability — grid operators may enforce cos φ ranges on your inverter).

## 6. Losses in wires and the voltage-choice argument

- Conductor loss: `P_loss = I² × R`. Wire resistance: `R = ρ × L / A` with copper resistivity **ρ ≈ 0.0175 Ω·mm²/m** at 20 °C (aluminum ≈ 0.028).
- **Voltage drop** (worst-case design check): `ΔU = I × R = I × ρ × L / A` for one conductor; **double it** for the full circuit (out and back):
  - DC or single-phase AC: `ΔU = 2 × L × I × ρ / A` (L = one-way length in m, A in mm²).
  - Rule: keep `ΔU < 2–3%` of system voltage (12 V → 0.24–0.36 V!). On 12 V this is brutally tight — main reason to go 24/48 V.
- **Worked example**: 240 W load (20 A) at 12 V over 5 m with 2.5 mm² cable: ΔU = 2 × 5 × 20 × 0.0175 / 2.5 = 1.4 V ≈ 12% — unacceptable. Same load at 24 V = 10 A → 0.7 V = 3%. At 48 V = 5 A → 0.35 V ≈ 0.7%. Consequence: at 12 V, use ≥6 mm² for any serious load or shorten the run.
- Implication for solar in general: transport a given power at the highest practical voltage (why panels are wired in series to 30–100+ V DC, why home batteries are ~48 V, why EV packs are 400–800 V).

## 7. Grid frequency as the balance signal

- European grid: nominal 50.0 Hz; frequency is identical everywhere in the synchronous area and reflects the instantaneous balance of generation and consumption.
- Critical thresholds (continent-wide protection cascade):
  - < **49.8 Hz**: load-shedding reserves activate
  - **49 Hz**: forced disconnection of consumers begins
  - **47.5 Hz**: generators separate from the grid (blackout)
  - **> 50.2 Hz**: generating plants are forcibly disconnected
- A large sudden generation/import deficit can drop frequency through these stages within seconds to minutes (Italy 2003 blackout: 57 million customers, restoration took 1–5 days). This is why anti-islanding and frequency-voltage ride-through rules exist for every grid-tie inverter.
- Typical central European supply reliability: ~12–16 min of unavailability per year (others up to 120 min/a) — the argument for backup power capability.

## 8. Units and conversions you will actually use

| From | To | Multiply by |
|---|---|---|
| Wh | kWh | × 0.001 |
| kWh | Wh | × 1000 |
| Wp (panel rating) | W (real, midday, cool) | × 0.7–0.8 |
| Ah at 12 V | Wh | × 12 |
| Ah at 24 V | Wh | × 24 |
| kWh | J | × 3.6 MJ |
| 1000 W/m² × h | 1 peak sun hour | identity |

- **Full-load hours**: annual energy ÷ nominal power. A 5 kWp array making 6000 kWh/a ran the equivalent of 1200 h at full power.
- **Peak sun hours (PSH)**: hours per day at 1000 W/m². Germany: Jan ≈ 1, Jul ≈ 5–6. See `solar-resource.md`.

## 9. Measurement (for commissioning and fault-finding)

- **Pyranometer**: professional irradiance sensor, <3% error. Cheaper: calibrated silicon reference cell (spectrally biased, but standard for PV surveys).
- DC clamp meter + a shunt-based battery monitor give you current truth; voltage alone lies (a deeply discharged battery can show 12 V+ when unloaded).
- Measure voltage drop under load, not open-circuit: a corroded connector passes open-circuit checks and fails under 20 A.
